Archival Recordings of David T. Leighton, Sr.

David T. Leighton, Sr. (1/21/1925 – 1/6/2017) served for many years as one of Admiral H. G. Rickover’s top assistants in the development of the Nuclear Navy. For his services, first as an active-duty officer in the USN, and later as a civilian working for Naval Reactors, he was recognized with the top awards offered by the Department of the Navy, the Department of Energy, and the Department of Defense.

1961 ASM Lecture

This is the recording of an invited lecture given by David T. Leighton, Sr. to the Golden Gate Chapter of the American Society for Metals in Oakland, CA, in January of 1961 on the status and development of the Nuclear Navy. This important historical document provides a fascinating account of both the history and difficulties encountered in the development of nuclear power for the Navy, with particular emphasis on submarines. At the time, then-Cdr. Leighton was the Naval Nuclear Power Superintendent of the Mare Island Naval Shipyard.

The ASM lecture was transcribed by the Naval Historical Foundation. The transcription is linked below and is best viewed while listening to the lecture:

1999 Interview

In addition to the historic 1961 lecture, we have recovered two other valuable recordings and transcribed them using modern AI tools. The first of these is an extensive phone interview between David Leighton, Sr. and his grandson James Leighton, done as a high school American History class project in 1999. While ordinarily such an interview might be expected to be superficial, it in fact provides a remarkable degree of insight into the choices which were made in the development of nuclear power for the Navy. Done in 1999, this would have been 19 years after his retirement from Naval Reactors and at age 74.

2012 MIT Seminar

Over a half-century after the ASM lecture, at age 87, he was invited to give an informal seminar to MIT students on his experiences with Admiral Rickover. In it he provides many anecdotes of his time working with Rickover, the philosophy of Naval Reactors, and of course the famous interview process — and why it was done that way.
